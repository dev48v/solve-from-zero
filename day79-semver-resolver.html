📦 A real semver resolver — ^1.2.3, ~1.2, 1.2.x, >=1.2 <2. The same strings go into npm, Cargo, Composer and pip, and this implements all four from their own documented rules over one shared version parser and comparator, so a difference in the grid is a difference in the rules rather than four parsers disagreeing by accident. 13 ranges × 19 versions is an enumerated 247-cell grid and every figure is a count. 🔴 Only two of the thirteen ranges mean the same thing in all four ecosystems — and both of them are the ones written out longhand: >=1.2 <2 and >=1.0.0. Every piece of shorthand is read differently somewhere. 🔴 The caret is not universal at all: pip has none, so ^1.2.3 selects nothing there while selecting five versions in npm. 🔴 And the prerelease rule catches people the other way round — ^1.2.3 does not select 2.0.0-rc.1 in any of them, even though 2.0.0-rc.1 sorts below 2.0.0, while ^1.0.0-alpha happily selects 1.2.0. Naming a prerelease in the range opens the door for that version tuple only.

One parser, one comparator, four rule-sets. Semver 2.0.0 precedence is implemented once — build metadata ignored, a prerelease sorting below the release, numeric identifiers below alphanumeric ones — and the four ecosystems are thin policies over it. That is deliberate: it means a disagreement in the table cannot be blamed on a parsing difference.

1. Only the longhand ranges are portable

The two that survive are the two with no shorthand in them. Everything else — the caret, the tilde, the .x wildcard, and a bare version number — is read differently by at least one of the four. If you want a range to mean the same thing to everybody, you have to write the inequality out. Across the whole grid the four give the same answer on 195 of 247 cells (78.95%), which sounds like broad agreement until you notice that 178 of those 195 are cells where all four say no. Only 17 cells in the whole grid are ones where every ecosystem agrees to select the version.

2. Where each pair comes apart

npm and Composer agree on every cell in this grid — on these thirteen forms, which is the honest scope of the claim rather than a statement that the two are the same in general. Cargo differs from both on five cells, all of them from one rule: a bare version is a caret range in Cargo, so 1.2.3 in a manifest means “1.2.3 up to but not including 2.0.0”, where npm and Composer read it as an exact pin. pip differs on around fifty, because it has no caret and no .x form at all.

3. The prerelease rule, which catches people both ways

🔴 ^1.2.3 does not select 2.0.0-rc.1, even though that version sorts below 2.0.0 and therefore below the range's upper bound. The rule is not about ordering: a prerelease is excluded unless the range itself names one. 🔴 And when the range does name one, the door opens wider than expected — ^1.0.0-alpha selects 1.0.0 and 1.2.0, because those are ordinary releases inside the caret window. The opt-in is for the tuple you named, not for prereleases in general.

What this covers, and what it does not

The four resolvers implement the forms that produce the disagreements above: caret with its zero-major special cases, tilde with its component-count dependence, .x wildcards, explicit inequalities, bare versions, and pip's ~=. They are not complete reimplementations. Out of scope, and each is another way for two tools to differ: compound ranges with ||, npm's --include=prerelease and Cargo's --precise, Composer's stability flags, pip's environment markers, and every resolver's behaviour when no version satisfies the range. The counts are over the thirteen ranges and nineteen versions in the grid, which is what they say.

Self-check

Runs on load. Several are controls: a claim that two ecosystems disagree means nothing without the cases where they agree, and the comparator is checked against the spec's own example ordering before anything else is measured.
