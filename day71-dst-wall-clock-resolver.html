SOLVEFROMZERO · DAY 71

🕰 IANA wall-clock resolver & calendar-arithmetic engine — a real TC39 Temporal / RFC 5545 resolver. Give it a zone, a wall clock and a duration and it returns the exact instant, naming the rule that decided it. The trap at the centre is not a value, it is a position: the same length of time written two ways — P1D or PT24H, P1DT1H or PT25H — lands on different instants, because date units are added to the wall clock and time units are added to the elapsed count, in that order and never mixed. Push on the asymmetry and it becomes absolute: over all 10,203 fold transitions in tzdata 2025b, the second occurrence of an ambiguous wall time is reachable by 0 date-unit durations and by all 10,203 time-unit ones. You can hold that instant, print it and parse it — you can never arrive at it by adding a day. 67 reference cases, every one a case a plausible implementation gets wrong, and the engine is raced against ICU's own offset strings across 418 zones and 20,436 transitions. The model everybody ships — a day is 86,400 seconds — is right on every one of the 245,232 cases away from a transition and wrong on 25.00% of the 490,464 next to one, which is exactly why it survives every test suite. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

FOUR IMPLEMENTATIONS, ONE INPUT

The first row is the specified ordering. The other three are the three shortcuts real codebases take. Away from a transition all four agree, always — which is why the shortcuts ship.

A duration is not a number. It is two numbers that are added in different spaces.

P1DT1H looks like a single quantity: 25 hours. It is not. The P half — years, months, weeks, days — is added to the wall clock, the digits on the face, and then the result is re-anchored to a real instant. The T half — hours, minutes, seconds — is added to the elapsed count, real seconds ticking past, and it never consults the calendar at all. Date half first, always.

Away from a DST transition the two spaces are the same space and nothing about this is observable. Next to one they come apart, and they come apart in the direction that is hardest to test for: the answer changes depending on which half of the duration string you wrote the hour in.

The rule to internalise. P1D means “tomorrow, same clock reading”. PT24H means “86,400 seconds from now”. These are different requests. Two days a year, in most of the world, they are different answers, and for the roughly 40% of humanity in a zone that never changes its clock they are identical forever — which is why the distinction survives code review.

“compatible” is not one policy. It is two, chosen by which way the clock jumped.

A wall time in a zone maps to 0, 1 or 2 instants. Two, and you must choose. Zero, and you must invent. The four policies are named compatible, earlier, later and reject, and three of those names mean what they say. The default does not:

situationcompatibleearlierlaterreject
fold — 2 instantsfirstfirstsecondthrows
gap — 0 instantsshift forwardshift backshift forwardthrows

Read the compatible column downwards. In a fold it behaves as earlier; in a gap it behaves as later. It always moves forward along the timeline of real instants, which is a coherent thing to want and is exactly what makes a repeating calendar event never run twice. But it means the default policy is a different policy depending on the sign of the offset change, and no caller is told which one fired.

And earlier in a gap is not the transition instant. It shifts the wall time back by the width of the gap and resolves that. New York 2025-03-09T02:30 under earlier is 01:30‑05:00, not 01:59:59 and not 03:00. Implementations that clamp to the transition instant are wrong by up to 59 minutes and pass every test written at 02:00.

The finding: half of every fold is unreachable.

Put the two rules together. Calendar arithmetic ends by resolving a wall time. Under the default policy, resolving a wall time in a fold returns the first of the two instants. Therefore no date-unit duration — no number of days, weeks, months or years, forwards or backwards, from any starting point — can ever land on the second one. That instant exists. It has a timestamp. It formats and parses. It is simply not in the image of the function.

Every fold in the database was checked: for each, the mid-fold wall time was resolved to its two instants, six date-unit routes were tried into the later one, and a single time-unit duration was tried as a control.

The control is the point. The instant is one PT1H away and always has been. Change P1D to PT24H in a scheduling library and a whole hour of the year stops being addressable, or starts being addressable, with no diff to the value and no change to the total length.

The mirror image is worse. Because compatible pushes forward out of a gap, arithmetic can land on instants that no user could have typed: New York 02:30 plus one day is 03:30, a clock reading the user never asked for. Gaps are surjective onto the wrong side; folds are not surjective at all.

The three shortcuts, scored

Three models, each of which a competent developer writes on purpose, each raced against the specified ordering over every transition in the database at four start offsets and six durations — 490,464 cases.

  • exact-ms — convert the whole duration to milliseconds and add. “A day is 86,400 seconds.”
  • wall-all — add every unit including hours to the wall clock, resolve once at the end. “Just do the arithmetic on the local time.”
  • time-first — the right two spaces, the wrong order: exact time, then calendar.

Note which cells are zero. exact-ms is perfect on every pure-time duration and wrong on half of every date duration. wall-all is its exact complement. time-first is wrong nowhere except on durations that mix the two halves — which is the only place the order can matter, and is therefore the one duration shape nobody writes a test for.

The 0.01% is the interesting one. wall-all disagrees on 5 of 81,744 P1DT1H cases — five, out of eighty-one thousand. A model that is wrong 0.006% of the time is a model that ships, survives, and produces one furious support ticket a decade with no reproduction.

Adding then subtracting does not return you

subtract mirrors add: the exact-time half comes off first, then the calendar half. Mirroring is the right choice — it makes x.add(d).subtract(d) work whenever no transition intervenes — but it cannot be an inverse, because the resolution step is many-to-one at every fold and undefined-to-one at every gap.

Nearly nine in ten of those failures pass through a gap or a fold. The rest are month clamping: 31 January plus one month minus one month is 28 January, and no transition is involved at all.

Midnight is not the start of the day

startOfDay is not T00:00:00. A zone can skip midnight, in which case the day begins later; and a zone can repeat midnight, in which case the day begins twice and you take the first.

Chile and Cuba do this every single year, on schedule, forever. So did Jordan, Syria, Lebanon, Egypt and Iran. Anything that computes a daily boundary by concatenating "T00:00:00" onto a date is wrong for 160 zones on 1,580 dates — and the error is silent, because 2018-11-04T00:00:00 in São Paulo still parses, still compares, and is simply an hour before the day starts.

The zones that break the assumptions you did not know you had

Five calendar dates in the database have no instants at all. Not a missing hour — the entire date. Pacific/Apia went from 29 December 2011 straight to 31 December. Anything that iterates dates by adding one day and formatting produces a date that never existed, and anything that resolves that date lands 24 hours away.

Three things I expected to find and did not

  • I expected the ±1-day probe to be provably broken. The resolution algorithm finds candidate instants by reading the offset one day either side of the nominal guess. Legal UTC offsets span −12:00 to +14:00 — twenty-six hours, wider than the probe. Pacific/Kiritimati really did move 24 hours and 40 minutes in one step. So the heuristic should be constructible-against. It is not: over 81,744 probes at four positions around every transition in the database, a ±1-day probe and a ±2-day probe returned identical candidate sets every time. The engine still ships the wider window, because “I could not construct a counterexample” is not “there is none” — but the claim I set out to make is not supported.
  • I expected Europe/Dublin to be observably different from Europe/London. tzdata models Irish Standard Time as the summer baseline with a negative saving applied in winter, the inverse of every other European zone. Real inversion, real consequence for anything reading an is-DST flag. But the offsets are identical to London on every instant, so a resolver that only ever asks for offsets — which is what this engine does, and what Intl exposes — cannot see it. The difference is real and this page cannot measure it; that is a limit of the instrument, and it is stated rather than papered over.
  • I expected timeZoneName:'longOffset' to be lossy. The whole justification for reconstructing offsets by subtraction was that ICU's ready-made offset string renders to whole minutes and would flatten Africa/Monrovia's −00:44:30. It does not: ICU 77.1 emits GMT-00:44:30, seconds intact, and the two derivations agreed on 40,872 of 40,872 samples. The real cost is arity — four string shapes out of one field, including a bare GMT for zero offset — which is a parsing hazard, not a precision one. I had written the opposite on this page before testing it, which is the whole reason the verifier exists.
  • A fourth, which did land. I assumed the specified until — calendar days off the wall clock, then a wall-clock remainder — would be the inverse of add. It is not: the remainder can itself straddle a transition, and it failed on 16 of 31,302 round trips. The fix is to seed the date units from the calendar and then correct them in instant space against the same anchor add uses. That version is inverse-exact by construction: 0 of 31,302.

The 67 reference cases

Every one chosen because a plausible implementation gets it wrong, and every one asserting the deciding rule, not only the answer.

Building it

About 380 lines, no dependencies, no embedded tz database, and — deliberately — no Date arithmetic anywhere. Every fact about a zone comes out of Intl.DateTimeFormat, which is the host's own IANA data; every fact about the calendar comes out of a proleptic Gregorian day-count. The two never mix.

The design decision that makes the rest fall out: the resolver returns candidates, not an answer. A function that returns an array of length 0, 1 or 2 forces every caller to confront the gap and the fold. A function that returns a timestamp lets them off, and that is why almost every library has this bug.

1. Reading an offset without an offset API

// Format the instant AS IF it were UTC-labelled wall fields, then subtract.
function getOffset(zone, epochMs) {
  var w = getWall(zone, epochMs);              // via Intl.DateTimeFormat
  return Math.round((wallToNominal(w) - Math.floor(epochMs/1000)*1000) / 1000);
}

The obvious shortcut is timeZoneName:'longOffset', which hands you "GMT-04:00" ready-parsed. I assumed it was lossy and it is not — ICU renders Africa/Monrovia's pre-1972 offset as GMT-00:44:30, seconds and all, and over 40,872 samples it agreed with the subtraction above on every single one. What it actually costs you is arity. Across 600 samples that one field emitted four different shapes — GMT, GMT+NN:NN, GMT-NN:NN and GMT-NN:NN:NN — and the zero-offset case is the bare string GMT with no sign and no digits at all, 42 of those 600. A regex written against the shape you happened to test returns NaN for UTC and drops thirty seconds in Monrovia, silently, in a function whose output is a number.

The subtraction survives for a different reason, and it is the better one: this engine has to read the wall clock anyway, so deriving the offset from that same reading makes the two self-consistent by construction. An offset parsed from a second, independent formatter call is one more thing that can disagree with the fields you are about to resolve against. The verifier uses longOffset precisely because it is that independent second opinion.

2. Candidates, not an answer

function possibleInstants(zone, wall) {
  var nominal = wallToNominal(wall);
  var oBefore = getOffset(zone, nominal - PROBE), oAfter = getOffset(zone, nominal + PROBE);
  var cands = oBefore === oAfter ? [nominal - oBefore*1000]
                                 : [nominal - oBefore*1000, nominal - oAfter*1000];
  return cands.filter(function (c) { return sameWall(getWall(zone, c), wall); });
}

Two guesses, then a verification round-trip: format each candidate back and keep only the ones that actually read as the requested wall time. The filter is the whole algorithm. Without it the function returns a plausible-looking instant for a time that never happened, which is precisely the failure everybody ships. With it, a gap returns [] and the caller cannot avoid noticing.

3. The disambiguation split, written as a split

if (poss.length > 1) {                       // FOLD
  var pick = (dis === 'later') ? poss[poss.length-1] : poss[0];
  //          compatible falls through to EARLIER here
}
// GAP: no candidates at all
var gapMs = (oAfter - oBefore) * 1000;
if (dis === 'earlier') return resolve(nominal - gapMs);
return resolve(nominal + gapMs);             // compatible falls through to LATER here

compatible is never named in either branch. It is the absence of later in one and the absence of earlier in the other, which is exactly what it is. Writing it as a third named case would require deciding what it means, and it does not mean anything on its own.

Note also that the gap branch shifts by gapMs and then resolves again rather than returning a computed instant. Lord Howe Island shifts by thirty minutes, Antarctica/Troll by two hours, Vostok once by seven, and five zones by a full day; a hard-coded hour is wrong for 147 of the 10,233 gaps in the database.

4. The ordering, and why it is two statements and not one expression

if (hasDate(dur)) {                                  // 1. wall-clock space
  var w1 = addCalendar(getWall(zone, epochMs), dur, overflow);
  r = resolve(zone, w1, dis);                        //    disambiguation fires HERE
  mid = r.epochMs;
}
var out = mid + timeMs(dur);                         // 2. exact-elapsed space

Two statements, in that order, with a resolution between them. Fuse them into one expression — add everything to the wall clock, resolve once — and you get the wall-all model, which is wrong on 18.78% of transition-adjacent cases. Swap them and you get time-first, wrong on 4.17%. The resolution step in the middle is not a formality: it is the only place the trace can record that a gap or a fold was entered, and disambiguationFired is the flag that lets the page count them.

5. A difference that is an inverse

var days = Math.trunc((bMs - anchor(months, 0)) / MS_DAY);        // seed from the calendar
while (days && sgn * (bMs - anchor(months, days))     <  0) days -= sgn;   // correct
while (       sgn * (bMs - anchor(months, days+sgn)) >= 0) days += sgn;   // in INSTANT space
var rem = bMs - anchor(months, days);                            // exact remainder

The first version of this counted calendar days on the wall clock and took a wall-clock remainder. It read better and it was wrong 16 times in 31,302, always where the remainder itself crossed a transition. The version above never asks what the clock says: it walks the date units against the very anchor() that add() will use, so add(a, until(a,b)) === b holds by construction rather than by luck.

6. A fast path that has to earn it

function getWall(zone, epochMs) {
  if (FAST_OK === null) FAST_OK = probeFast();   // sv-SE format() vs formatToParts, 42 cells
  if (FAST_OK) { var w = getWallFast(zone, epochMs); if (w) return w; }
  return getWallSlow(zone, epochMs);             // formatToParts, always correct
}

formatToParts allocates thirteen objects per call and this engine makes hundreds of thousands of calls. sv-SE renders ISO-shaped 2025-11-02 01:30:00 from format() and is about 4.7× cheaper — but locale output is not a contract. So the fast path is used only after it has been proven equal to the slow one on a probe grid that includes the epoch, a fold, a seconds-resolution LMT offset and a whole-day skip, and any string it cannot parse falls straight through. The in-page suite re-checks the two paths against each other on every run.

7. Verifying it

Four layers, none of which trusts the engine:

  • ICU's own offset strings as the oracle. The engine reconstructs offsets by subtracting a formatted wall clock from an instant. The check reads timeZoneName:'longOffset' and parses ICU's own GMT±HH:MM[:SS] string directly — no calendar arithmetic, no subtraction, a different code path to the same tzdata. Raced over every zone and every transition. Same shape of check as Day 69's ICU race and Day 70's live Apache.
  • An interval classifier that never probes. Gap and fold windows are re-derived from the transition endpoints alone — read the wall clock one second before the jump and at the jump, and the two readings bound the window — then wall times are classified by plain interval comparison. It shares no line with possibleInstants and cannot agree for the wrong reason.
  • Round trips as properties, not samples. resolve(getWall(t)) === t for every unambiguous instant; add(a, until(a,b)) === b for every largest-unit; the fast and slow formatter paths against each other.
  • 67 reference cases from the specification's prose and the database's own oddities, each asserting the deciding rule and not only the timestamp.
