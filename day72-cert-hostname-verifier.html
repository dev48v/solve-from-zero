SOLVEFROMZERO · DAY 72

🔐 Certificate hostname verifier — a real X509_check_host(). Give it a hostname and a certificate and it says MATCH or MISMATCH and names the clause that decided it, under three profiles: OpenSSL 3.2.1, Node 22's tls.checkServerIdentity, and RFC 9525 as written. Everyone knows what *.example.com means. Almost nobody knows what stops *.com — and it is not a public suffix list, because neither library contains one. It is a dot count: the star needs at least two dots to its right. So *.com is dead and *.co.uk is live, matching bbc.co.uk in both real implementations, and so are *.github.io, *.appspot.com and *.s3.amazonaws.com. 🔴 The mechanism is worse than a rejection: OpenSSL never rejects a malformed wildcard, it demotes the star to an ordinary character and carries on comparing strings — of the 103 starred patterns in the differential corpus it reads 26 as wildcards and silently turns 77 into literals, refusing 0, while Node reads 52 as wildcards, demotes 24 and refuses the remaining 27, and RFC 9525 allows 20. Same certificates, three partitions, no error either way. 🔴 And the CN is not deprecated, it is disabled by type: an email, an iPAddress or a URI SAN leaves it authoritative and only a dNSName silences it, so 7 of the 31 SAN-bearing certificates in the exhaustive 64-certificate slot corpus are accepted purely on the CN. Every verdict on this page is checked against the real thing: a throwaway certificate authority issues 282 leaves, 234 of them carrying the two differential grids, and every one is raced across 8,480 cells against openssl verify -verify_hostname and Node's own checkServerIdentity — 0 disagreements with either, over 25,703 verifier assertions and 312,415 in the page. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

Browsers carry a suffix list of their own and CAs are forbidden by the Baseline Requirements from issuing these — but that is policy above the verifier, and a private CA, an internal PKI or a pinned self-signed certificate is not subject to it.

FOUR MENTAL MODELS, SCORED

Each of these is a matcher a competent engineer writes on purpose, raced against the real algorithm over the grid above and over the exhaustive 64-certificate slot corpus. The zeroes are the tell: every model is exactly right on the half of the problem it was designed for, which is why it survives review.

Verification is not a comparison. It is a search for an entry of the right type.

The mental model almost everyone carries is a string compare with a bit of glob on top: take the name in the certificate, take the name in the URL, see if they line up. The real algorithm never does that. It walks the subjectAltName list in certificate order, and for each entry it asks a question before comparing anything: is this entry of the type I am checking? A dNSName when the client asked about a hostname; an iPAddress when it asked about an IP literal. Entries of any other type are skipped without being read.

That skip is the whole design, because a second variable is being set while it happens: san_present. It is set only by entries of the matching type. And at the end of the loop, san_present is what decides whether the Common Name is ever looked at.

So the CN is not deprecated by the code. It is disabled by a neighbour. Give a certificate one dNSName that does not name your host and the CN is dead. Give it an email address, an IP or a URI instead — entries that say nothing about hostnames at all — and the CN is authoritative again. The rule is not "does this certificate have a SAN", it is "does this certificate have a SAN of this type", and the two are different on every certificate that has one and not the other.

What that costs, counted exhaustively

Take one hostname and build every certificate that could plausibly name it: five candidate SAN slots — a matching dNSName, an unrelated dNSName, an iPAddress, an email, a URI — in all 25 = 32 subsets, crossed with a CN that either names the host or does not. Sixty-four certificates, no sampling, every one of them issued for real and put in front of openssl verify.

The model everybody states out loud — if there is a SAN, the CN is ignored — is wrong on 7 of the 64, and every one of its errors is a false reject: it predicts a mismatch where the real library says MATCH. A model that under-accepts fails safe in production and never gets caught, which is exactly why it is still being repeated.

The reason this is not a museum piece. A public CA cannot issue a certificate with no dNSName SAN any more. An internal PKI can, a self-signed service certificate usually does, and openssl req -x509 with no -addext produces one by default. On every one of those, in curl, in Python's ssl, in nginx's upstream verification and in PHP, the field deciding who you are talking to is still the Common Name.

The finding: what stops *.com is a dot count.

Ask anyone why a certificate authority will not sell you *.com and you will hear about public suffixes. That is a real rule and it lives in the CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements and in the browsers, both of which carry the Public Suffix List. It is not in the verifier. Here is the entire guard, the last two lines of OpenSSL's valid_star(): 

if ((state & (LABEL_START | LABEL_HYPHEN)) != 0 || dots < 2)
    return NULL;

dots is the number of dots in the whole pattern. Two or more and the star is an operator. One and it is not. Node states the same rule differently — if (patternParts.length <= 2) return false; — and lands in exactly the same place. Neither file mentions a registry, a suffix, or a list.

*.com and *.uk have one dot and are refused. *.co.uk, *.org.uk, *.github.io and *.appspot.com have two and are wildcards, in both libraries, matching every second-level name under those suffixes. The line between "obviously insane" and "fine" is not how much of the internet does this cover. It is how many dots did you type.

And the same count runs the other way. *.s3.amazonaws.com has three dots, so it is a perfectly ordinary wildcard covering every bucket-shaped hostname on that service. Nothing in the verifier knows that the second label is a tenancy boundary. Nothing can: the boundary is a fact about the operator's policy and the certificate carries no field for it.

A refused wildcard is not a refused certificate

valid_star() returning NULL does not mean "reject". Look at what the caller does with it: 

star = valid_star(pattern, pattern_len, flags);
if (star == NULL)
    return equal_nocase(pattern, pattern_len, subject, subject_len, flags);
return wildcard_match(...);

A pattern OpenSSL will not treat as a wildcard falls through to a literal string compare. The star stops being an operator and becomes an ordinary character in a hostname. *.com is now a certificate for the single host whose name is the four characters *.com — which is not a name DNS can carry, so the certificate authenticates nothing at all, and says so with a plain hostname mismatch rather than "your wildcard is invalid".

Node has no such fallback. The moment a pattern contains a star it does not like, it returns false. Across the corpus that difference is not marginal:

Two shipping implementations, the same certificates, and they do not even agree on how many of them contain a wildcard. Node demotes too, just far less often and for one specific reason: a first label beginning xn-- is matched by its exact spelling and nothing else, and that escape fires before the star-count and label-count rules, so xn--a*b — a single label, no dots at all — is a valid identity for the host literally spelled xn--a*b. The demoted reading also explains the strangest cells in the grid: OpenSSL matches *.example.com against the literal hostname *.example.com, and matches f*o.example.com against f*o.example.com, because in the second case the star was never an operator to begin with.

Adding a letter next to the star makes the star weaker — and looser

Inside wildcard_match() there is one conditional that everything else hangs off: 

if (prefix_len == 0 && *suffix == '.') {
    if (wildcard_start == wildcard_end)   /* the star ate nothing */
        return 0;
    allow_idna = 1;
    ...
}

Read the guard: it fires only when the star is the entire first label. In that case the star must consume at least one character, and A-label hosts (xn--…) become matchable. Put a single literal character to its left — f*.example.com — and both clauses switch off. The star may now expand to zero characters, so f*.example.com matches f.example.com; and the A-label protection disappears, so a partial wildcard is refused against xn-- hosts by a separate line further down.

The one question the API cannot be asked. The mirror case — does *.example.com match the empty-label host .example.com? — is unreachable from outside, because a leading dot switches X509_check_host() into an entirely different mode: it becomes a client-side suffix pattern meaning "any name under this domain", and never reaches the wildcard code at all. Internally the answer is no. Node, which has no such mode, answers the question directly and says yes. Both facts are asserted on this page; only one of them could be raced against the real binary.

The trailing dot: the same name, two answers

example.com. and example.com are the same name. The trailing dot is the DNS root label; resolvers strip it, it reaches the same server, and new URL("https://example.com./") keeps it in the hostname. OpenSSL does not strip it, on either side, so a trailing dot on the host is a mismatch and a trailing dot in the certificate makes that identity match nothing. Node strips it — and strips it twice, because checkServerIdentity() calls unfqdn() and then splitHost() calls unfqdn() again, so example.com.. is accepted and example.com... is not.

Of the 148 cells in the 8,160-cell differential grid where OpenSSL and Node give different verdicts, 142 — 95.9% — are decided by a dot: a trailing one on the host, a trailing one in the certificate, or an empty label somewhere in between. The entire remainder is the f*o reading and the A-label rule.

🔴 What the measurement killed

Four things this page intended to say did not survive being raced against the real libraries, and one of them was a claim about the specification itself.

  • "RFC 9525 is strictly stricter than OpenSSL." False. It is looser on 16 of 650 cells — every one of them because the specification normalises a trailing dot away and OpenSSL does not. The assertion on this page now measures the exception rather than denying it.
  • "f*o.example.com is a partial wildcard everywhere." False, and it was a bug in this engine until the differential caught it. OpenSSL refuses a star with a literal on both sides — if (!atstart && !atend) return NULL; — and demotes it. Node has no such rule and treats it as a wildcard. Three real hosts flipped.
  • "Node strips one trailing dot." It strips two. Seven grid cells disagreed with the real checkServerIdentity until unfqdn() was applied twice, which is what the shipped code does by accident of composition.
  • "*.example.com matches .example.com, so a full star can eat nothing." The observation was real and the explanation was wrong: the match came from the leading-dot suffix pattern mode, not from the wildcard. The same pattern matched *.a.b.example.com too, which no wildcard rule permits.
  • "Node refuses every A-label wildcard." It matches their exact spelling, and the escape sits above the star-count and label-count rules rather than below them. That ordering is only visible from three certificates — xn--a*b matches itself while a*b does not, and xn--b9-b1**an matches itself while x**y.example.com does not — and no amount of reading the algorithm from the outside would have produced it.
  • And one failure that was not the spec's at all. The reference disagreed with the engine on exactly one pattern, the bare star, and the engine was right: the OpenSSL binary on this machine is a MinGW build whose C runtime globs its own argv, so a hostname of * was being expanded into a list of filenames before X509_check_host ever saw it. The verifier now runs every comparison from an empty directory. A differential test can be wrong about the reference, not only about the thing under test.
What did survive, and is worth carrying. Whether a name matches a certificate is not a property of the name and the certificate. It is decided by the slot the identity sits in, by the types of its neighbours, by the number of dots to the right of a star, and by whether the star has a literal character beside it. Four positional facts, none of them about the characters being compared.

The engine

The block between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END in this file's source is DOM-free and is the whole verifier: three profiles, OpenSSL's six X509_CHECK_FLAG_* bits, the corpora, the mental models and the assertion suite. Nothing in it touches document, which is how the external verifier can lift it out and race it against the real binaries without a browser.

1. The type check that decides everything

for (i = 0; i < san.length; i++) {
  e = san[i];
  if (e.type !== checkType) { walk.push({... "wrong type, skipped"}); continue; }
  sanPresent = true;                       // <- set ONLY by the matching type
  ...
}
if (sanPresent && !(flags & F.ALWAYS_CHECK_SUBJECT)) return { match:false, ... };
// only now is the CN reachable

Two statements, and the order between them is the rule. Moving sanPresent = true above the type test — which is what "if there is a SAN, ignore the CN" means as code — changes the verdict on exactly the certificates whose SAN entries are all of the wrong type, and on no others.

2. valid_star, a state machine that answers a yes/no question

if (c === "*") {
  var atstart = (state & LABEL_START) !== 0;
  var atend   = (i === p.length - 1) || p.charAt(i + 1) === ".";
  if (star >= 0 || (state & LABEL_IDNA) || dots) return -1;   // one star, first label, not xn--
  if (!atstart && !atend) return -1;                          // no foo*bar
  star = i;
}
...
if (state & (LABEL_START | LABEL_HYPHEN)) return -1;
if (dots < 2 && !(flags & F.SINGLE_LABEL_SUBDOMAINS)) return -1;

Four of the five refusals are about the star's neighbours, not the star. The fifth is the dot count. SINGLE_LABEL_SUBDOMAINS is the flag that turns the dot count off — it exists so that an internal PKI can write *.corp, and switching it on makes *.com a working wildcard.

3. The expansion is policed after the fact

var mid = host.slice(wStart, wEnd);
if (mid === "*") return true;                      // the star may match a literal star
for (var i = 0; i < mid.length; i++) {
  var c = mid.charAt(i);
  if (!((c >= "0" && c <= "9") || (c >= "A" && c <= "Z") ||
        (c >= "a" && c <= "z") || c === "-" || (allowMulti && c === "."))) return false;
}

This is where "one label" actually lives. The star is not restricted while it expands; it is allowed to swallow the rest of the name and then the swallowed text is checked for a dot. Set MULTI_LABEL_WILDCARDS and the single character . joins the allowed set, at which point *.example.com covers a.b.c.example.com and the whole subtree with it.

5. Verifying it

Four layers, none of which trusts the engine:

  • Real certificates, real OpenSSL. The external verifier generates a throwaway EC certificate authority, issues 234 leaf certificates carrying the corpus in their real subjectAltName extensions, and asks openssl verify -CAfile ca.pem -verify_hostname <name> — the actual X509_check_host() that curl, Python and nginx call. Every certificate is verified once without a hostname first, so any failure that is not error 62: hostname mismatch aborts the run instead of being read as a mismatch. Same shape of check as Day 61's git check-ignore, Day 69's ICU and Day 70's live Apache.
  • A second, independently written implementation. The same DER, parsed by crypto.X509Certificate, handed to Node's own tls.checkServerIdentity. It is not a port of OpenSSL and it disagrees with it, which is the point: an engine that matched only one of them could be matching a transcription error.
  • Oracles written from the RFC, not from the engine. Wildcard matching is re-derived in the verifier as a label-array walk, the SAN-type rule as a set predicate, and the dot rule by counting; each is compared cell for cell rather than spot-checked.
  • Properties, not samples. Case-insensitivity over every letter in both directions; a non-matching entry never blocks a later matching one, in either order; an empty certificate never matches anything; every accept names a source; and RFC 9525 never accepts what OpenSSL refuses except for a trailing dot — the exception is asserted with its exact count rather than waved away.

6. What to do about it

  • Do not read a wildcard's safety off its shape. *.co.uk and *.example.com are the same construct to every verifier on this page. If you pin or allow-list certificates, check the suffix yourself against a real list.
  • Set X509_CHECK_FLAG_NEVER_CHECK_SUBJECT if you are calling OpenSSL directly and you want the modern rule. It is the only way to make "the CN does not count" true, and it is off by default.
  • Normalise the trailing dot before you compare anything — on the host, and in whatever you feed to a certificate request. It is the single most common source of a verification that works in a browser and fails in curl.
  • Detect IP literals yourself. X509_check_host("10.0.0.1", ...) happily matches a dNSName spelled 10.0.0.1. The routing to X509_check_ip() is the application's job, and the library will not do it for you.
