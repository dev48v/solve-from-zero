🔁 RRULE recurrence expander — a real RFC 5545 §3.3.10 engine. Type FREQ=MONTHLY;BYDAY=-1FR and it lists the dates, and for any date it did not produce it names the rule part that decided. The trap at the centre is not a token that means two things — it is a token that does two things: BYMONTHDAY=13 under FREQ=MONTHLY generates the 13th of every month, and the identical BYMONTHDAY=13 under FREQ=DAILY throws days away. Same nine characters, opposite operation, decided entirely by a rule part sitting somewhere else — and BYDAY flips the same way when you merely add a neighbour beside it. The spec prints this as a 9×7 table of Expand and Limit; here it is one filter over a window whose width is FREQ, and all 58 of the table's cells are then measured rather than trusted. 56 reference cases, 609 assertions, and four wrong engines scored on 850 generated rules: the natural "BYxxx filters the stream" model is exactly correct for FREQ=DAILY — 0 of 200 — and wrong on 92% of YEARLY rules, which is why it survives every test anybody writes. 56 REFERENCE CASES


      


    

  






  

    


      
One line of text, and nobody can tell you what dates come out.


      
An RRULE is short enough to read aloud and dense enough that two conforming
      implementations can disagree. It is the string behind every calendar invitation, every cron
      replacement that needed the last working day of the month, every "repeats monthly"
      checkbox. And almost every hand-rolled expander is wrong in the same place.


      
1. The same rule part is a generator or a filter, depending on FREQ


      
FREQ=MONTHLY;BYMONTHDAY=13   → the 13th of every month.  BYMONTHDAY GENERATES.
FREQ=DAILY;BYMONTHDAY=13     → the 13th of every month.  BYMONTHDAY FILTERS.


      
Identical output. Opposite mechanism. In the first, the period is a whole month and
      BYMONTHDAY picks a day out of it. In the second, the period is a single day and
      BYMONTHDAY throws away twenty-nine of every thirty periods. RFC 5545 prints this
      as a table with two words in it — Expand and Limit — and the
      cell you land in is chosen by FREQ, not by the part you wrote.


      
The reason this is not an academic distinction:


      
FREQ=MONTHLY;BYMONTHDAY=2,15   → TWO dates a month.
FREQ=DAILY;BYMONTHDAY=2,15     → two dates a month, from sixty periods.


      
Still equal. Now take the part away:


      
FREQ=MONTHLY                   → one date a month, on DTSTART's day-of-month.
FREQ=DAILY                     → every day.


      
An implementation that treats every BYxxx as a filter over the FREQ stream produces the
      right answer for both of the first two lines and for every FREQ=DAILY rule ever
      written. This page measures how far that gets you: 0 of 200 generated DAILY
      rules wrong, and 276 of 300 YEARLY ones. The model is not approximately
      right. It is exactly right in the corner where people test.


      
2. BYDAY changes meaning because of its neighbours


      

        
FREQ=YEARLY;BYDAY=FR is every Friday of the
        year — about 52 dates. Add one more part and the same
        BYDAY=FR stops generating and starts filtering:
        FREQ=YEARLY;BYDAY=FR;BYMONTHDAY=13 is Friday the 13th. The
        part you added was BYMONTHDAY. The part that changed meaning was BYDAY.

      


      
This is the whole content of Note 2 under the spec's table, and it is why an
      implementation cannot decide what a rule part does by looking at the rule part.


      
The ordinal moves too. BYDAY=20MO under FREQ=YEARLY is the
      twentieth Monday of the year. Add BYMONTH=5 and the ordinal is now
      counted inside the month, so the equivalent rule is BYDAY=3MO. Same
      syntax, different scope, and no error either way.


      
3. An absent rule part is not a wildcard


      
FREQ=MONTHLY            → BYMONTHDAY is DEFAULTED from DTSTART. One date a month.
FREQ=MONTHLY;BYDAY=TU   → the default is GONE. Every Tuesday of every month.


      
Adding a part deletes a default you never wrote. The rule is all-or-nothing: name any of
      BYWEEKNO, BYYEARDAY, BYMONTHDAY or BYDAY
      and none of the others are filled in from DTSTART any more. An engine that skips this step
      turns FREQ=MONTHLY into every day of every month — a bug that is
      obvious in one line of output and is measured here at 178 of 850 rules.


      
4. DTSTART on the 31st is a trap with no error


      
DTSTART:19970131   FREQ=MONTHLY
→ 31 Jan, 31 Mar, 31 May, 31 Jul, 31 Aug …   February and April never occur.


      
RFC 5545 has no roll-over. A day the month does not have simply produces nothing, and
      because the rule is still valid there is nowhere for an error to appear. "Monthly on the
      31st" quietly means "eight times a year".


      
RFC 7529 added the missing knob fifteen years later: SKIP=BACKWARD clamps to
      the last day of the month instead of skipping it, which is what every calendar UI has been
      doing all along. Both are on the EXPAND tab, and they disagree on 4 of 10
      sample rules — including 29 February, which is either an event that happens roughly
      every four years or an annual one, depending on a rule part that is not in the string.


      
5. WKST is not decoration


      
DTSTART:19970805   FREQ=WEEKLY;INTERVAL=2;COUNT=4;BYDAY=TU,SU;WKST=MO
→ 5 Aug, 10 Aug, 19 Aug, 24 Aug
                                                              WKST=SU
→ 5 Aug, 17 Aug, 19 Aug, 31 Aug


      
The RFC puts this example in the specification itself, because the result is hard to
      believe. INTERVAL=2 skips alternate weeks, and which days are in a week
      depends on where the week starts. Move the boundary and different days fall on the near side
      of it.


      
It reaches BYWEEKNO as well, where week 1 of a year is the first week with at
      least four days in that year. Under WKST=MO, week 20 of 1998 starts 11 May;
      under WKST=SU it starts 17 May — and the years on either side are
      unaffected. The drift is not a constant offset you can correct for.


      
6. BYSETPOS indexes a set you never see


      
DTSTART:19970904   FREQ=MONTHLY;BYDAY=TU,WE,TH;BYSETPOS=3   → 4 Sep, 7 Oct, 6 Nov


      
September's third Tuesday-Wednesday-or-Thursday is the 4th — counted from the 2nd
      and the 3rd, which are before DTSTART and are not occurrences. BYSETPOS runs on the period's
      full candidate set before DTSTART, UNTIL and COUNT are applied. Apply the range
      first and September answers the 9th.


      
It is also the only way to say "the second-last working day of the month". No combination
      of ordinals can express it, because the ordinal attaches to a single weekday and this
      question is about a set of five.


      
7. The parts that quietly cannot co-occur


      
    
        
  • COUNT and UNTIL in the same rule — forbidden, and
        commonly written anyway when a rule is edited from "10 times" to "until December".
    • 
        
  • BYWEEKNO with anything but FREQ=YEARLY.
    • 
        
  • BYMONTHDAY with FREQ=WEEKLY — there is no coherent
        answer, so the spec removes the question.
    • 
        
  • An ordinal BYDAY such as 2MO under FREQ=WEEKLY:
        the ordinal needs a month or a year to count inside, and a week is neither.
    • 
        
  • BYSETPOS with no other BYxxx part — there is no set to index.
    • 
      


      
UNTIL has one more rule that costs an hour when it bites: it is
      inclusive, and it compares the whole instant. UNTIL=19970905T080000Z
      on a rule whose occurrences are at 09:00 stops on the 4th, not the 5th.


      
What an RRULE is not


      
    
        
  • Not cron. Cron ORs its day-of-month and day-of-week fields when both
        are restricted, so 13 * FRI means the 13th or any Friday. RRULE ANDs
        them: BYMONTHDAY=13;BYDAY=FR is Friday the 13th. Same two fields, opposite
        connective.
    • 
        
  • Not the whole recurrence set. A calendar entry may carry several
        RRULEs plus RDATEs and EXDATEs; this page expands one rule.
    • 
        
  • Not timezone-aware. Everything here is naive wall-clock arithmetic on
        integers — no Date object is constructed anywhere in the engine, so no
        timezone can move a result. A real calendar server has to add DST on top, which is where
        "the 09:00 meeting is now at 10:00" comes from.
    • 
      


    

  






  

    


      
🔨 How it is built


      
1. The design decision that removes the table


      
The obvious way to implement §3.3.10 is to write the table down: two functions per
      rule part, an expander and a limiter, and a switch on FREQ to pick one. That is 9×7
      cells of branching, and the two Notes make four of them context-dependent.


      
There is a cheaper description. Give every period a window of candidate
      days whose width is decided by FREQ, then run one filter over it:


      
YEARLY   → every day of the year      (365 candidates)
MONTHLY  → every day of the month     (28-31)
WEEKLY   → the 7 days of the week, aligned to WKST
DAILY    → one day


      
Expand and limit stop being two operators. BYMONTHDAY=13 against a 365-day
      window keeps 12 days, which is an expansion; against a 1-day window it keeps 0 or 1, which is
      a limit. The behaviour the spec spends a table describing falls out of the window width, and
      the funnel on the EXPAND tab is literally that array shrinking.


      
2. No Date object anywhere

      
function toDays(y, m, d){        // Howard Hinnant's civil-calendar algorithm
  const yy = y - (m <= 2 ? 1 : 0);
  const era = Math.floor(yy / 400);
  ...
  return era * 146097 + doe - 719468;   // 1970-01-01 is day 0
}
function weekday(dn){ return ((dn % 7) + 10) % 7; }   // 0 = Monday

      
Dates are integers and instants are seconds. new Date("1997-09-02") and
      new Date(1997, 8, 2) disagree about timezone, and a recurrence engine that
      crosses a DST boundary with local-time arithmetic loses or gains an hour silently. Integer
      day numbers cannot.


      
3. The default that is easy to miss and easy to measure

      
if (!byWeekNo && !byYearDay && !byMonthDay && !byDay){
  if (freq === "YEARLY"){ if (!byMonth) byMonth = [dt.m]; byMonthDay = [dt.d]; }
  else if (freq === "MONTHLY") byMonthDay = [dt.d];
  else if (freq === "WEEKLY")  byDay = [{ n: 0, wd: weekday(dtDay) }];
}

      
Four parts guard the block and one of them being present cancels the whole thing. Write
      the condition as four separate ifs and FREQ=MONTHLY;BYDAY=TU still
      picks up BYMONTHDAY from DTSTART, which turns "every Tuesday" into "Tuesdays
      that fall on the 2nd".


      
4. One filter, returning an index rather than a boolean

      
function rejectAt(dn, win){        // -1 = the day survives
  if (byMonth && byMonth.indexOf(c.m) < 0) return 0;
  ...
  if (byDay && !byDayOk(dn, win))         return 4;
  return -1;
}

      
An index, not a name, because the funnel re-runs the filter stopping after part k
      to count how many days each part removed. The same call answers "is this a match" for the
      expander and "which part rejected it" for the explainer, so the two can never drift apart and
      say different things about the same date.


      
5. The ordinal's scope is a function of the rule, not the part

      
function ordinalScope(dn, win){
  if (win.kind === "Y" && !byMonth) return theYear(dn);   // BYDAY=20MO
  return theMonth(dn);                                    // BYDAY=1FR
}

      
Four lines, and the only place the two Notes under the spec's table actually cost
      anything. Hard-code the month and FREQ=YEARLY;BYDAY=20MO silently returns
      nothing, since no month has twenty Mondays — measured here at 40 of 850 rules.


      
6. BYSETPOS runs before the range, not after

      
const set = windowSet(win);          // the period's FULL candidate set
...
for (const v of set){
  if (v < start) continue;           // DTSTART applied AFTER BYSETPOS
  if (until !== null && v > until) break;
  out.push(v);
}

      
Filter by DTSTART first and BYSETPOS counts a different set in the first period only,
      which is exactly the kind of bug that passes review and then produces one wrong date in
      September 1997.


      
7. Verified three ways, none of them "it looks right"

      
    
        
  • 56 reference cases, most of them lifted from the worked examples in
        §3.8.5.3 of the RFC itself, so the expected dates are not this engine's opinion.
    • 
        
  • A differential test against a different shape of implementation. The
        verifier carries a per-day membership predicate — walk every date in a
        window and ask "does this one satisfy the rule", using JS Date arithmetic that
        the engine does not use at all. Two implementations that share no code agreed on
        54,063 dates across 256 rule/DTSTART pairs.
    • 
        
  • The spec's own table, measured. All 58 non-N/A cells are checked by
        adding the part and removing it again with everything else fixed. 57 agree.
    • 
      


      
🔴 The cell that does not agree, and why it is left alone

      
One cell of the measured table disagrees with the spec: BYDAY under YEARLY when
      BYWEEKNO is present, which Note 2 calls a special expand and the
      measurement calls a limit. The disagreement is real and structural. In the RFC's staged
      algorithm the intermediate set after BYWEEKNO is a set of weeks, and BYDAY expands
      each week into days. In a day-window model the week already exists as seven days, so BYDAY
      can only cut. The two models are observationally identical — the differential test and
      the RFC's own week-20 example both pass — but their intermediate cardinalities are not,
      because one of them never builds a set of weeks. Reporting it as an expand would have meant
      writing a special case whose only purpose was to make a number agree.


      
🔴 One of the reference cases was wrong, not the engine

      
The WKST=SU variant of the BYWEEKNO example was written expecting 1999 to shift by a week.
      The engine said 1998 shifts and 1999 does not. Working the four-day rule by hand for all
      three years agreed with the engine: 1 January 1998 is a Thursday, so under WKST=SU only three
      days of that week fall in 1998 and week 1 starts on 4 January — a week later than under
      WKST=MO. 1997 and 1999 land the same way under both. The case now asserts the correct dates
      and the page says the drift is not monotonic, which is the more useful claim anyway.


      
8. What the 56 reference cases cover

      
BYMONTHDAY expands under MONTHLY and limits under DAILY - same 5 dates out
BYDAY expands under YEARLY alone, limits the moment BYMONTHDAY appears
20MO counts inside the YEAR; add BYMONTH and it counts inside the MONTH
FREQ=MONTHLY alone defaults BYMONTHDAY from DTSTART; BYDAY=TU deletes the default
the 31st skips February entirely - and clamps to it under RFC 7529
WKST=MO vs WKST=SU on the RFC's own INTERVAL=2 example: 2 of 4 dates move
BYWEEKNO week 1 is the first week with 4 days in the year, so 1999 jumps
BYSETPOS counts candidates that precede DTSTART and are not occurrences
UNTIL is inclusive and compares the whole instant, not the date
BYHOUR expands under DAILY and limits under MINUTELY
COUNT+UNTIL, BYWEEKNO under MONTHLY, ordinal BYDAY under WEEKLY: all refused

      
Each one is a case where a plausible implementation returns something else, which is the
      only reason to write a reference case at all.
