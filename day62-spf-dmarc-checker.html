📧 SPF / DKIM / DMARC Evaluator — paste a mock DNS zone and a raw message, and a hand-written email-authentication engine runs all three protocols live: a real RFC 7208 check_host() that walks the terms left to right and stops at the first match, with + - ~ ? qualifiers, ip4/ip6 CIDR containment, a/mx/ptr/exists/include, redirect=, full macro expansion, and the 10-lookup budget counted the way the RFC actually counts it · a real RFC 6376 DKIM pass that implements simple and relaxed canonicalisation for headers and body and hashes the result with WebCrypto SHA-256, so one stray trailing space breaks bh= in front of you · and RFC 7489 DMARC with organizational-domain identifier alignment, p/sp/pct and the resulting disposition · it demonstrates why SPF alone stops nothing a human can see, why forwarding breaks SPF but not DKIM, and why a chain of include: that trips the lookup limit is a permerror that can never pass DMARC · vanilla JS, no network, nothing leaves the page

SPF authenticates an address the recipient never sees. That is the whole problem DMARC exists to fix.

There are two "from" addresses in every email. The envelope sender — MAIL FROM, spoken during the SMTP conversation, used for bounces, invisible in every mail client on earth — and the From: header, which is the only one a human reads. SPF checks the first one. So an attacker can register a domain, publish a perfectly valid SPF record for it, send from an IP that record authorises, collect a clean spf=pass, and still put your name in the From: header. DKIM has the opposite shape: it signs the message itself, survives forwarding, and carries its own d= domain — which also does not have to match From:. DMARC is the small piece of glue that makes the other two mean something: it demands that at least one of them pass and be aligned with the visible From:. Everything below is computed in your browser, right now, against the zone in the box.

TermCosts?Why

RFC 7208 §4.6.4 names exactly six terms that consume the budget of ten. Everything else is free, because it is answered from the record already in hand. Exceeding ten is a permerror — not a soft failure, not a warning — and under DMARC a permerror can never be an aligned pass.

What this page does not do

Two honest limits. First, there is no DNS: the zone box above is the resolver, which is what makes the page reproducible and lets you build a broken domain in ten seconds. Second, DKIM here verifies the body hash and both canonicalisations and stops there — the b= value is an RSA or Ed25519 signature over the canonicalised header set, and checking it needs the public key from selector._domainkey.d plus real asymmetric crypto. That half is arithmetic; the half implemented here is where DKIM actually breaks in production. A footer added by a mailing list, a gateway that rewrites a subject, an appliance that strips a trailing space — every one of those changes bh=, and the signature is dead long before anybody looks at b=. The page still checks that the key record exists and is not revoked, because a published-then-deleted selector is the other half of the same bug.

Six things about email authentication that generate the support tickets

1. SPF authenticates the envelope, not the From: a human reads. MAIL FROM is spoken during the SMTP conversation and never rendered by a mail client. An attacker who owns examp1e-mail.net can publish a flawless SPF record for it, pass SPF from their own server, and still write Ada <ada@example.com> in the header. Load the Lookalike spoof scenario: SPF says pass and DMARC still says reject, because the passing identifier is not the one on screen. SPF on its own has never stopped a single display-name phish. 2. Plain forwarding breaks SPF and leaves DKIM intact. A forwarder connects from its own IP carrying the original MAIL FROM, so the origin domain's record does not list it and the answer is fail — through no fault of anyone. DKIM signs the message, so it survives the hop untouched. That asymmetry is the entire reason DMARC accepts either an aligned SPF pass or an aligned DKIM pass rather than demanding both. 3. +all is a disaster and it looks like success. It says "every IP address on the internet is authorised to send as me". Every check returns pass, dashboards go green, and the record is worth strictly less than having none — a missing record at least yields none, which receivers treat with suspicion. 4. Eleven lookups is a permerror, and a permerror can never pass DMARC. The budget is ten terms, not ten records, and it is spent by include, a, mx, ptr, exists and redirect anywhere in the tree, including inside every vendor's include. Add one more SaaS sender and a working domain becomes unauthenticatable overnight; if DKIM is not also aligned, DMARC fails outright. 5. ~all and -all differ less than you think — under DMARC. Standalone, -all asks receivers to reject and ~all asks them to accept and mark. But DMARC only cares whether SPF passed: softfail and fail are equally not-a-pass, and the disposition comes from p=, not from the SPF qualifier. ~all plus p=reject rejects. 6. A d= that is not aligned passes DKIM and still fails DMARC. Any domain can sign any message. A bulk sender signing with d=mail.vendor.net produces a genuine, cryptographically valid pass that does nothing for a From: at your domain, because relaxed alignment compares organizational domains and vendor.net is not example.com. This is why vendors ask you to CNAME a selector into your own zone.

🔨 Build it yourself

    
An email-authentication engine is three small machines sharing one input. A resolver you can fake, so the whole thing is testable without the internet. An SPF evaluator that is a left-to-right loop with a budget, a recursion rule and a macro language nobody expects. And a DKIM canonicaliser that reconstructs the exact byte sequence the signer hashed — which is the part that breaks in production, and the part you can verify in a browser with no keys at all. DMARC is thirty lines on top that finally connect the other two to the address a human reads. Here it is, step by step.

    
    

      
  1. 
        
    1
    Fake the resolver first — and model both kinds of nothing
    
        
    Every SPF bug is a DNS bug wearing a costume, so the resolver has to be data you control. A zone is a map from name to record types. The subtlety that matters is that DNS has two ways of saying no, and SPF treats them differently from a timeout: NXDOMAIN (the name does not exist) and NOERROR with zero answers (the name exists but holds no record of that type) are both void lookups, capped at two, while a genuine timeout or SERVFAIL is a temperror — a retryable maybe, not a verdict. Conflating the three is how engines end up rejecting real mail during a DNS outage.
    
        
    const zone = {
  "example.com":      { TXT:["v=spf1 mx -all"], MX:[{pref:10, host:"mail.example.com"}] },
  "mail.example.com": { A:["192.0.2.25"] },
  "slow.example.com": { TIMEOUT:true }
};

function dnsQuery(zone, name, type){
  const n = String(name).toLowerCase().replace(/\.$/, "");
  const node = Object.prototype.hasOwnProperty.call(zone, n) ? zone[n] : null;
  if (node === null) return { rcode:"NXDOMAIN", records:[], isVoid:true  };
  if (node.TIMEOUT)  return { rcode:"TIMEOUT",  records:[], isVoid:false };
  const recs = node[type] || [];
  return { rcode:"NOERROR", records:recs, isVoid: recs.length === 0 };
}

    2
    Parse the record into terms — mechanisms and modifiers are different animals
    
        
    A record is v=spf1 followed by whitespace-separated terms. A term whose leading run of letters is followed by = is a modifier (redirect=, exp=, or an unknown one you must ignore rather than reject); everything else is a mechanism with an optional leading qualifier. Mechanisms are positional and modifiers are not, which produces two surprises: a second redirect= is a permerror even though the first may never run, and a modifier written anywhere still applies at the end. Note the dual-CIDR syntax on a and mx: /24 sets the IPv4 prefix, //64 the IPv6 one, and a:host/24//64 sets both.
    
        
    function parseSpf(txt){
  const toks = String(txt).trim().split(/[ \t]+/).filter(Boolean);
  if (!toks.length || toks[0].toLowerCase() !== "v=spf1")
    return { error: 'record does not start with "v=spf1"' };
  const rec = { terms:[], redirect:null, exp:null, hasAll:false };
  for (let i = 1; i < toks.length; i++){
    const t = toks[i];
    const mod = t.match(/^([A-Za-z][A-Za-z0-9._-]*)=(.*)$/);
    if (mod){ addModifier(rec, mod[1].toLowerCase(), mod[2]); continue; }
    let q = "+", s = t;
    if ("+-~?".indexOf(s.charAt(0)) >= 0){ q = s.charAt(0); s = s.slice(1); }
    const name = (s.match(/^[A-Za-z][A-Za-z0-9]*/) || [""])[0].toLowerCase();  // ip4/ip6
    rec.terms.push(parseMechanism(q, name, s.slice(name.length)));
    if (name === "all") rec.hasAll = true;
  }
  return rec;
}

// "/24"      -> { p4:24, p6:null }
// "//64"     -> { p4:null, p6:64 }
// "/24//64"  -> { p4:24, p6:64 }        anything else is a permerror

  3. 
        
    3
    CIDR containment, both families, no library
    
        
    "Is this address inside this block" is one masked comparison. For IPv4, pack the four octets into a 32-bit integer and compare under a mask — but beware the shift: 0xFFFFFFFF << 32 is not zero in JavaScript, because the shift count is taken modulo 32, so /0 has to be special-cased or it silently becomes /32. For IPv6, skip BigInt and compare eight 16-bit words: whole words while at least sixteen bits of prefix remain, then one masked word. Both run in constant time and neither allocates.
    
        
    function ip4InCidr(ip, net, prefix){
  const a = ip4ToInt(ip), b = ip4ToInt(net);
  if (a === null || b === null || prefix < 0 || prefix > 32) return false;
  if (prefix === 0) return true;               // 0xFFFFFFFF << 32 === 0xFFFFFFFF
  const mask = (0xFFFFFFFF << (32 - prefix)) >>> 0;
  return ((a & mask) >>> 0) === ((b & mask) >>> 0);
}

function ip6InCidr(ip, net, prefix){
  const a = ip6ToWords(ip), b = ip6ToWords(net);      // 8 x 16-bit words
  if (!a || !b || prefix < 0 || prefix > 128) return false;
  let bits = prefix;
  for (let i = 0; i < 8; i++){
    if (bits <= 0) return true;
    if (bits >= 16){ if (a[i] !== b[i]) return false; bits -= 16; continue; }
    const m = (0xFFFF << (16 - bits)) & 0xFFFF;
    return (a[i] & m) === (b[i] & m);
  }
  return true;
}

  4. 
        
    4
    check_host() — left to right, first match wins
    
        
    This is the opposite of the rule that governs a .gitignore, and confusing the two produces a genuinely wrong mental model. SPF evaluates terms strictly in order and returns on the first mechanism that matches; the qualifier on that mechanism becomes the result. Everything after it is never evaluated — which is why -all written near the front disables the entire rest of the record, and why the DNS lookups of later terms are never spent. If no mechanism matches, the record falls through to redirect= when there is one, and otherwise to neutral.
    
        
    const QUAL = { "+":"pass", "-":"fail", "~":"softfail", "?":"neutral" };

for (const term of rec.terms){
  if (term.kind !== "mech") continue;          // modifiers are handled after
  const m = matchMechanism(term, st);          // may recurse, may throw
  if (m.match) return { result: QUAL[term.qual], by: term };   // STOP HERE
}
if (rec.redirect !== null && !rec.hasAll) return followRedirect(rec, st);
return { result: "neutral" };                  // the documented default

  5. 
        
    5
    The budget of ten — counted along the path actually taken
    
        
    RFC 7208 §4.6.4 names six terms that cost a lookup: include, a, mx, ptr, exists and the redirect= modifier. ip4, ip6 and all cost nothing because they are answered from the record already in hand, and exp= costs nothing because it is fetched after evaluation finishes and only when the result is fail. Two details decide whether your counter is right. The budget is global across the whole recursion, not per record — one counter threaded through every include. And it counts terms evaluated, not terms written: a term sitting after the winning match never runs and never costs anything. Exceed ten and the answer is permerror, full stop.
    
        
    function spend(st, what){              // ONE shared counter for the whole tree
  st.lookups += 1;
  if (st.lookups > 10) throw { spf:"permerror", why:"over the 10-term limit" };
}

// a, mx, ptr, exists, include, redirect  -> spend(st)
// ip4, ip6, all                          -> free
// exp                                    -> free (fetched after the verdict)

// separate sub-budgets, RFC 7208 4.6.4:
//   mx   : at most 10 address lookups for the MX names
//   ptr  : at most 10 PTR names examined
//   void : at most 2 lookups returning NXDOMAIN or an empty answer

    6
    include is a recursive call with a translation table
    
        
    This is the most misread mechanism in the specification. include:vendor.net does not splice the vendor's terms into your record and it does not adopt the vendor's qualifiers. It runs a full nested check_host() against the vendor's domain and then flattens seven possible results into "matched" or "did not match". Only pass counts as a match. A nested fail, softfail or neutral all mean the same thing to you — keep going — so the vendor's -all is invisible from outside. But a nested none (the vendor deleted their record) or permerror becomes your permerror, which is exactly how one vendor's mistake takes your domain down.
    
        
    const INCLUDE_MAP = {          // RFC 7208 section 5.2, verbatim
  pass:      "match",
  fail:      "no-match",
  softfail:  "no-match",
  neutral:   "no-match",
  temperror: "temperror",      // propagate
  permerror: "permerror",      // propagate
  none:      "permerror"       // a MISSING record is YOUR error
};

function evalInclude(term, ctx, st){
  spend(st, "include");
  const target = expandMacro(term.domain, ctx);
  const inner  = checkHost(st, subContext(ctx, target), st.depth + 1);
  const out    = INCLUDE_MAP[inner.result];
  if (out === "match")    return { match:true  };
  if (out === "no-match") return { match:false };
  throw { spf: out, why: "include:" + target + " returned " + inner.result };
}

    Macros — SPF has a string language and almost nobody knows
    
        
    Any domain in a, mx, ptr, exists, include, redirect= and exp= may contain macros. %{i} is the client IP, %{s} the whole envelope sender, %{l} its local part, %{o} its domain, %{d} the domain currently being evaluated, %{h} the HELO and %{v} the literal string in-addr or ip6. The transformers are the interesting half: a digit keeps the rightmost N parts, r reverses them, and trailing punctuation redefines the split characters. So %{ir}.%{v}._spf.%{d2} turns 192.0.2.25 at mail.example.com into 25.2.0.192.in-addr._spf.example.com — a per-IP lookup computed at query time. An uppercase letter URL-escapes the result, which only ever matters inside exp= text.
    
        
    function expandOne(spec, ctx){
  const m = /^([slodipvhcrt])([0-9]*)([r]?)([-.+,\/_=]*)$/i.exec(spec);
  if (!m) throw { spf:"permerror", why:"bad macro %{" + spec + "}" };
  let parts = split(macroValue(m[1].toLowerCase(), ctx), m[4] || ".");
  if (m[3]) parts = parts.reverse();                        // "r"
  const n = m[2] ? parseInt(m[2], 10) : 0;                  // digits
  if (n > 0 && parts.length > n) parts = parts.slice(-n);   // RIGHTMOST n
  const out = parts.join(".");                              // always rejoined with "."
  return (m[1] >= "A" && m[1] <= "Z") ? urlEscape(out) : out;
}

// %%  -> "%"      %_  -> " "      %-  -> "%20"
// and if the expansion exceeds 253 chars, drop whole labels from the LEFT

    8
    DKIM canonicalisation — the bytes that were actually hashed
    
        
    DKIM does not sign the message; it signs a canonical rendering of the message, and there are two of them for headers and two for the body. simple means "change nothing", which is honest and unbelievably brittle — one whitespace edit anywhere in transit kills it. relaxed lowercases field names, unfolds continuation lines, collapses runs of spaces and tabs to a single space, strips trailing whitespace and removes the space around the colon. Both body algorithms drop trailing empty lines and add a final CRLF when the body has content without one. The asymmetry worth memorising: an empty body canonicalises to CRLF under simple and to the empty string under relaxed, so the same nothing produces two different hashes.
    
        
    function relaxHeader(raw){
  const i = raw.indexOf(":");
  const name = raw.slice(0, i).trim().toLowerCase();
  const v = raw.slice(i + 1)
               .replace(/\r\n/g, "")           // unfold, keeping the folding WSP
               .replace(/[ \t]+/g, " ")        // collapse every WSP run to one SP
               .replace(/^[ \t]+|[ \t]+$/g, "");
  return name + ":" + v + "\r\n";
}

function relaxBody(body){
  const lines = toCRLF(body).split("\r\n").map(function(l){
    return l.replace(/[ \t]+/g, " ").replace(/[ \t]+$/, "");
  });
  const b = lines.join("\r\n").replace(/(?:\r\n)+$/, "");   // drop trailing blanks
  return b === "" ? "" : b + "\r\n";      // simple returns "\r\n" for an empty body
}

    The header set, the emptied b=, and the hash you can check
    
        
    The signed header set is exactly the list in h=, in that order, each name selecting the last unused instance of that field counting from the bottom — which is what lets a signature survive a forwarder prepending another Received:. The DKIM-Signature field is then appended to the hash input with two rules that trip everyone: its b= value is emptied while the tag itself is kept, and the field is contributed without a trailing CRLF. The body is separate: canonicalise it, truncate to l= octets if present, hash, base64, compare to bh=. That comparison needs no key and no crypto beyond SHA-256, and it catches almost every real DKIM failure.
    
        
    function stripBTag(raw){                  // "b=abc" -> "b=" , leaves "bh=" intact
  const c = raw.indexOf(":");
  const parts = raw.slice(c + 1).split(";");    // b= is base64: never holds a ";"
  for (let i = 0; i < parts.length; i++){
    const m = parts[i].match(/^([ \t\r\n]*)b([ \t\r\n]*=)/);
    if (m) parts[i] = m[1] + "b" + m[2];
  }
  return raw.slice(0, c + 1) + parts.join(";");
}

    10
    DMARC — organizational domains, alignment, disposition
    
        
    DMARC adds one idea and one lever. The idea is alignment: an authentication result only counts if the domain it authenticated is the domain in From:. strict means the labels are identical; relaxed — the default for both adkim and aspf — means the two share an organizational domain, found by taking the longest public suffix plus one more label. That is why mail.example.com aligns with example.com under relaxed but not strict, and why example.co.uk needs a suffix list rather than "the last two labels". The lever is policy: the receiver queries _dmarc.<From domain>, falls back to _dmarc.<org domain>, and if it landed on the org record for a subdomain then sp= overrides p=. The verdict itself is a single OR.
    
        
    function orgDomain(d, psl){
  const L = d.toLowerCase().replace(/\.$/, "").split(".");
  let n = 0;                                         // labels in the matched suffix
  for (let i = 0; i < L.length; i++)                 // ascending i = longest first
    if (psl.indexOf(L.slice(i).join(".")) >= 0){ n = L.length - i; break; }
  if (n === 0) n = 1;                                // unknown TLD -> last label
  return L.slice(L.length - Math.min(L.length, n + 1)).join(".");
}

const spfOK  = spf.result === "pass" && aligned(envelopeDomain, fromDomain, rec.aspf);
const dkimOK = sig.result === "pass" && aligned(sig.d,          fromDomain, rec.adkim);
const pass   = spfOK || dkimOK;                       // EITHER one. never both.

const policy   = (foundAtOrg && isSubdomain && rec.sp !== null) ? rec.sp : rec.p;
const applied  = pass ? "none" : (rec.pct > 0 ? policy : downgrade(policy));
const residual = pass ? "none" : (rec.pct >= 100 ? policy : downgrade(policy));

    Prove it — golden vectors, a second evaluator, and a full cross-product
    
        
    There is no git check-ignore for email, so the oracle has to be built — and three sources of genuine external truth are available. The RFC 6376 canonicalisation examples in §3.4.6 are printed character by character with <SP> and <HTAB> markers, so they are exact vectors nobody can fudge; the published SHA-256 of an empty body under each algorithm is another. For SPF, write a second evaluator with a different shape — a table-driven recursive walker against the loop — and run both over thousands of generated zones, asserting the result and the final lookup count. For DMARC the input space is small enough to enumerate completely: every combination of SPF result, SPF alignment, DKIM result, DKIM alignment, p, sp, pct and subdomain-or-not is a few thousand cells, so check them all rather than sampling.
    
        
    // RFC 6376 3.4.6 example 1 -- external ground truth, not self-invented
const RAW = "A: X\r\nB : Y\t\r\n\tZ  \r\n\r\n C \r\nD \t E\r\n\r\n\r\n";
assert(relaxHeaders(RAW)         === "a:X\r\nb:Y Z\r\n");
assert(relaxBody(bodyOf(RAW))    === " C\r\nD E\r\n");
assert(simpleBody(bodyOf(RAW))   === " C \r\nD \t E\r\n");
assert(simpleHeaders(RAW)        === "A: X\r\nB : Y\t\r\n\tZ  \r\n");

// the published empty-body hashes, both algorithms
assert(sha256b64(simpleBody("")) === "frcCV1k9oG9oKj3dpUqdJg1PxRT2RSN/XKdLCPjaYaY=");
assert(sha256b64(relaxBody(""))  === "47DEQpj8HBSa+/TImW+5JCeuQeRkm5NMpJWZG3hSuFU=");

// differential SPF: same zone, two independent engines, same answer AND same count
for (const [zone, query] of generated(5000)){
  const a = engine.evalSpf(zone, query), b = referenceWalker(zone, query);

}
